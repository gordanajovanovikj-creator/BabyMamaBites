Choking vs. gagging: how to be prepared

Gagging is a normal part of learning to eat. Choking is an emergency. Know the difference, lower the risk, and know what to do.

5 min read

In this article
  1. Calm, prepared and confident
  2. Gagging is normal
  3. Why babies gag
  4. Lower the risk
  5. Be ready before you need it
  6. Choking is an emergency

In short

  • Gagging is loud, red and normal: stay calm and let your baby clear it.
  • Choking is often silent: act right away and call 911.
  • Change high-risk foods, keep your baby upright, and take an infant CPR class.

Calm, prepared and confident

Few things make a parent’s heart skip faster than seeing their baby cough, sputter or turn a little red while eating. When you’re introducing solid foods, watching your baby handle new textures can feel nerve-wracking.

Knowing the difference between gagging and choking, and knowing basic first aid, helps you stay calm at the table and let your baby explore food with confidence.

Gagging is normal

Gagging is a healthy part of learning to eat. A baby’s gag reflex sits much further forward on the tongue than an adult’s, so when a piece of food is too big or unfamiliar, the reflex pushes it forward and out of the way.

  • What it looks like: watery eyes, a red or pink face, coughing, gagging or retching sounds, and leaning forward a little.
  • What to do: stay calm and keep your baby sitting upright. Don’t put your fingers in their mouth; that can push food further back. Reassure them calmly, for example, "You’re okay, you’re working it out," and let them clear it themselves.

Why babies gag

In babies, the gag reflex starts near the middle to front of the tongue and moves farther back as they grow. Gagging helps keep food from reaching the airway before it has been chewed enough. Gagging is loud and red; choking is silent.

Lower the risk

You can’t remove every risk, but safe food preparation and good mealtime habits lower the chance of choking a lot.

  • Change high-risk foods: never serve whole grapes, cherry tomatoes or hot dogs, or large chunks of meat. Cut grapes and cherry tomatoes lengthwise into quarters, and cut hot dogs and other tube-shaped foods into thin strips, then small pieces.
  • Skip hard and sticky foods: keep raw carrots, popcorn and whole nuts away from babies and toddlers. Never give nut butter by the spoonful; thin it with water, breast milk or yogurt, or spread it thinly.
  • Always sit upright: no eating while lying down, crawling, walking, or riding in the car or stroller. Strap your baby into a high chair with their feet supported.
  • Always watch: stay with your baby the whole time they eat, even for a few seconds.

Be ready before you need it

We strongly encourage every parent and caregiver to take an in-person infant CPR and first aid class. The American Red Cross and the American Heart Association offer them. Preparation turns fear into confidence, so your baby can explore food with freedom and joy.

Choking is an emergency

Choking happens when food or an object blocks the airway. A choking baby is often silent: they can’t cry, cough, breathe or make a sound; may be gasping or wheezing; may look panicked; or their skin, lips or face turn pale, blue or dusky.

Act right away and call 911. This is a medical emergency. If your baby is under 1, these are the basic steps taught in infant first aid classes. They don’t replace hands-on training.

  • Get help: if someone is with you, have them call 911 right away. If you’re alone, shout for help, and if you can, call 911 on speaker so you can keep your hands free.
  • 5 back blows: lay your baby face down along your forearm, resting on your thigh, with their head lower than their chest. Support the head and jaw with your hand. With the heel of your other hand, give up to 5 firm blows between the shoulder blades.
  • 5 chest thrusts: turn your baby face up along your forearm, still supporting the head and keeping it lower than the chest. Place 2 fingers in the center of the chest, just below the nipple line, and give up to 5 quick thrusts straight down, about 1 1/2 inches deep.
  • Repeat: keep alternating 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts until the object comes out and your baby can cry, cough or breathe.
  • Only remove an object you can see. Never sweep your finger blindly in your baby’s mouth.
  • If your baby stops responding: start infant CPR. If no one has called 911 yet, call after about 2 minutes of CPR.
  • Even if your baby seems fine afterward, call your pediatrician to get them checked.
