Introducing common allergens

Why offering egg, peanut and other allergens early can help, and how to do it.

6 min read

In short

  • There's no benefit to delaying common allergens: offer them early, then regularly.
  • Start with a small taste, one allergen at a time, early in the day.
  • Call 911 for trouble breathing, lip or tongue swelling, repeated vomiting or unusual sleepiness.

Early can help

In the US, nine foods cause about 90% of food allergy reactions: milk (dairy), egg, peanut, tree nuts, wheat, soy, sesame, fish and shellfish.

Families used to be told to wait until age 2 or 3. Newer research found no benefit to delaying allergens. The LEAP study found that offering peanut foods early, starting between 4 and 11 months, and then regularly, greatly lowered the chance of peanut allergy in babies at high risk.

How to start

  • Start with a few low-allergy foods first, like infant cereal or pureed fruit.
  • Then offer the common allergens one at a time: egg, peanut, dairy (like yogurt), wheat, soy, sesame, fish, shellfish and tree nuts.
  • Start with a small taste. If there's no reaction, gradually offer more.
  • Offer a new allergen early in the day, like at breakfast or lunch, so you can watch your baby for the rest of the day, and wait 3 to 5 days before the next one.
  • Once a food is tolerated, keep it in the diet regularly.
  • Always use a safe form: peanut and nut butters thinned, never chunks or whole nuts; egg fully cooked.

Practical ways to prepare them

  • Peanut: mix 1 teaspoon of smooth, unsalted peanut butter with 2 teaspoons of warm water, breast milk, formula or puree. Never give peanut butter straight off a spoon, and never whole peanuts.
  • Egg: cook it well, as a fully set scramble or a hard-boiled egg, and mash it well.
  • Tree nuts: stir finely ground nut flour (like almond flour) or a thinned smooth nut butter into warm oatmeal or fruit puree.

Talk to your pediatrician first if

  • Your baby has severe, persistent eczema
  • Your baby has had an allergic reaction to a food, such as egg
  • Food allergies run in your family (you can also talk to an allergist)

Watch for reactions

Food allergies can be severe and life-threatening. After a new food, watch for signs like a rash, vomiting or diarrhea. If you see them, stop the food and call your pediatrician.

Call 911 for trouble breathing, swelling of the lips or tongue, hives all over, repeated vomiting, or unusual sleepiness. If your baby has been prescribed epinephrine, give it right away.

Most egg allergies are caused by proteins in the egg white, but the yolk can cause reactions too.
