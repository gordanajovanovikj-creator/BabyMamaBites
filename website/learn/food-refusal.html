When your baby refuses food

Refusing new foods is normal. Here's how to keep offering.

3 min read

In short

  • Refusal is normal; it often means a food is unfamiliar, not disliked.
  • It can take 8 to 10 tries, so offer again calmly, next to a food your baby likes.
  • You decide what, when and where; your baby decides whether and how much.

Refusal is normal

When your baby turns their head, pushes the plate away or keeps their mouth shut, it’s easy to feel worried or frustrated. Food refusal is usually a healthy sign of independence and of your baby listening to their appetite. Refusing a food often means it’s unfamiliar, not that they dislike it.

It takes time

Your baby may not like a food the first time. It can take 8 to 10 tries before a young child accepts a new food.

Try this

  • Wait about a week, then offer the food again.
  • Mix the new food with one your baby already likes.
  • Eat the food yourself first; babies learn by watching.
  • Give a simple choice between two foods.
  • Freeze small portions so repeat tries don't waste food.
  • Always put one familiar food your baby likes next to a new or previously refused food.
  • Keep your tone calm and even: skip big cheers for a bite and disappointment when they don’t, so food doesn’t become about approval.

Phases are normal

Toddlers may suddenly refuse foods they used to love, or not want foods to touch. These phases often pass by about age 5. Talk to your pediatrician if you're worried about growth.

Who decides what

A widely used approach (from the Ellyn Satter Institute) splits mealtime jobs:

  • You decide what food is served, when and where.
  • Your baby decides whether to eat and how much.

Plan it, cook it, track it

Week-by-week plans, recipes and a food log in the Mama & Baby Bites app. Coming soon to iPhone.

Every child is different

Every child's relationship with food develops in its own way. These are supportive ideas, not clinical solutions. If your child strongly refuses food, is losing weight or has trouble swallowing, talk to your pediatrician or a pediatric feeding specialist.
